# End-to-end: Block AttnRes in a 1.3B decoder

Generated by `scripts/summarize_model.py` from
`results/model_bfloat16.json`. Reproduce with `python bench/bench_model.py`.

The operator microbenchmark answers how fast the kernel is. This answers the
question that actually matters: **how much of a training step does the residual
mechanism cost, and how much of that does fusing it remove?**

NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Max-Q Workstation Edition, bfloat16, batch 4 x seq 2048. 24 layers (48 sublayers), d_model 2048, d_ff 5632, 8 AttnRes blocks, vocab 32768.

`standard` is an ordinary PreNorm residual and is the control. The two AttnRes
modes differ *only* in which operator implements the mechanism: same weights,
same data path, same sequence attention, and parameter counts equal by
construction and asserted in `tests/test_model.py`. `stack` builds the source
tensor with `torch.stack` at every site, as the paper's pseudocode does; `arena`
writes each source once into a preallocated buffer.

| variant | params | fwd ms | bwd ms | step ms | tokens/s | peak GiB |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| standard | 1.300B | 128.86 | 262.61 | 428.89 | 19101 | 30.33 |
| attnres/stack | 1.301B | 176.43 | 480.15 | 704.59 | 11627 | 38.62 |
| attnres/arena | 1.301B | 168.82 | 489.85 | 704.54 | 11627 | 31.87 |
| switchyard/stack | 1.301B | 152.08 | 301.99 | 492.61 | 16630 | 38.61 |
| **switchyard/arena** | 1.301B | 141.97 | 304.05 | **484.21** | 16918 | 31.86 |

## What the mechanism costs

Two independent estimates. **Control difference** subtracts the standard-residual
step time and so captures everything the mechanism adds, plumbing included.
**Profiler** attributes time to the operator's own `record_function` regions,
which is narrower and misses backward work that autograd spreads across generic
nodes -- for `attnres/stack` it cannot be attributed at all, which is itself why
the control difference is the number quoted.

| variant | added ms | % of step (control diff) | % of step (profiler) |
|---|---|---|---|
| attnres/stack | 275.7 | 39.1% | not attributable |
| attnres/arena | 275.7 | 39.1% | 34.2% |
| switchyard/stack | 63.7 | 12.9% | 7.4% |
| switchyard/arena | 55.3 | 11.4% | 7.4% |

Block AttnRes costs **39% of a training step** with the framework
implementation and **11%** with the fused one -- a
3.4x reduction in the mechanism's overhead. End to end that is
**1.46x** the step time,
705 ms down to 484 ms, and
**1.46x** the tokens per second.

Quoting the Amdahl arithmetic rather than the kernel speedup is deliberate.
The operator is 3.5-5x faster in isolation, but it is one part of a step, so
the end-to-end figure is smaller and it is the honest one.

The arena saves **6.75 GiB** of peak memory against stacking
(38.61 to 31.86 GiB). The paper's pseudocode stacks at
every site, which at eight blocks copies most of the residual history again
per sublayer:
265 slab copies per forward against 49, where a slab is one
`B x T x D` tensor (32 MiB here).

Against the standard-residual control, AttnRes implemented properly adds
1.53 GiB of peak
memory (5%)
and 11% of step time. Those are
the numbers an architect deciding whether to adopt it would want.

The source-count schedule the model actually produces was checked against the
paper's Eq. 6 at run time, not just in tests.

## Training smoke test

Small config, fp32, overfitting one fixed batch for 300 steps. This is not a
quality claim; it checks that a systems change did not break learning.

| variant | loss | finite | uniform at init |
|---|---|---|---|
| standard | 8.4294 -> 0.0063 | True | -- |
| attnres | 8.4291 -> 0.0061 | True | True |
| switchyard | 8.4291 -> 0.0061 | True | True |

All three overfit and none diverges. The AttnRes runs start from exactly
uniform depth attention, as the zero-initialized pseudo-queries require
(max deviation 9.9e-09).

After 300 steps the depth attention has moved but not collapsed: mean
normalized entropy 0.990 of `ln N`, mean maximum
weight 0.360, and 0.343 of
the mass still on the token embedding. That is an observation on a
deliberately degenerate task, not a finding -- anything more would need
real training, which is out of scope here.

## Two GPUs

Block AttnRes is a local tensor operation; nothing about it needs cross-device
communication, and inventing some so this section could exist would be
contrived. So the two-GPU work asks the two questions that are worth asking:
does the operator still train correctly under DDP, and where does scaling stop
on a machine whose cards are connected by PCIe at a measured 25.8 GB/s with no
NVLink.

| variant | 1 GPU tok/s | 2 GPU tok/s | speedup | efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| standard | 17555 | 29799 | 1.70x | 85% |
| switchyard | 16238 | 28138 | 1.73x | 87% |

**Gradients are identical across ranks after all-reduce** -- maximum
deviation 0.0e+00. That is the check that matters here: a custom
autograd Function returning a wrong or rank-dependent gradient for one of its
inputs can pass every single-GPU test and still corrupt a distributed run.
The fused operator's backward, and the arena's in-place staging, both survive
it.

Scaling holds up better than the interconnect would suggest -- 85-87% rather
than the collapse a 25.8 GB/s link and a ~2.6 GB bf16 gradient all-reduce might
imply -- because NCCL overlaps the reduction with the backward pass. The fused
variant scales marginally *better* (87% against 85%), which is not a win worth
claiming: it has slightly more compute per byte communicated, so there is more
backward to hide the all-reduce behind.
